Quantities of Interest for Interactions

Researchers routinely test whether a treatment effect differs across groups. They much more rarely say which causal quantity they are testing. This site documents dcameaipe, an R package that makes that choice explicit — and that estimates the chosen quantity without assuming the treatment effect is a straight line.

The package accompanies Quantities of Interest for Interactions and the Pitfalls of Assuming Linear Treatment Effects (Serrano-Serrat 2026, Sociological Science). It fits a model in which each group gets its own flexible function of the treatment, and returns two distinct estimands — the D-CAME and the AIPE — alongside the conventional linear interaction coefficient, so the three can be read side by side.

·Two ways to ask the same question

Suppose a treatment's effect is non-linear, and two groups are exposed to it at different typical intensities. You can compare the groups the way the data actually finds them — each at its own typical dose — or you can hold the dose fixed and compare the groups on equal footing. These are different questions, and under non-linearity they give different answers.

When the effect curve is a straight line, it does not matter which comparison you run — the two coincide. When it bends, and the groups are exposed at different intensities, the two panels can disagree, sometimes in sign. Naming which one your research question calls for is the first contribution of the paper; estimating it without assuming linearity is the second.

In the paper's empirical application the gap is not subtle. A conventional linear interaction reports 0.11, the D-CAME is 0.33 — three times larger — and the AIPE is indistinguishable from zero. All three come from the same data, and each answers a different question. See An Application.
