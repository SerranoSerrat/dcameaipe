Open threads: what happens when the non-linearity sits somewhere else, what controls do to the quantity of interest, and how the framework changes when the treatment takes a handful of discrete values.

4.1Three places non-linearity can hide

“Non-linear interaction model” is used loosely for at least three distinct situations, and they call for different fixes. It is worth separating them before going further.

  • Non-linear treatment effects. $E[Y \mid D, X]$ bends in $D$: the effect of one more unit of treatment depends on how much treatment you have already had. This is what the paper is about, and it is what drives a wedge between D-CAME and AIPE.
  • Non-linear moderation. The conditional marginal effect bends in $X$: the standard product term $\beta^{L}(D \times X)$ forces the effect of $D$ to move linearly across levels of the moderator. This is the problem Hainmueller, Mummolo and Xu (2019, HMX) set out to solve with binning and kernel estimators. §4.2 takes it up.
  • Non-linear link functions. Logit, probit, Poisson and the rest impose non-linearity in the treatment by construction, through compression — marginal effects vary with $D$ and with every covariate even when no product term appears in the index. The estimand logic carries over, but the covariate bookkeeping does not, and it deserves its own treatment. Left for a future iteration of this document.

These are independent. A model can be linear in the treatment and non-linear in the moderator, or the reverse — and the standard interaction specification assumes away both at once.

4.2Non-linear moderators

The estimand exists, but the summary is missing

HMX and the subsequent literature deliver the conditional marginal effect as a function: $\theta(x) = E[\partial Y_i(d)/\partial d \mid X_i = x]$, plotted across the support of the moderator with pointwise and uniform confidence bands. In the vocabulary of Chapter 2, that curve is the CAME evaluated at each moderator value.

What that literature does not supply is a summary of the interactive effect itself. A plotted curve tells you the effect is larger at high $X$ than at low $X$; it does not give you a number for “how much larger, on average” with an interval attached, and readers end up eyeballing whether two bands overlap. The natural summaries are already available in this framework: the D-CAME as a contrast between representative moderator values, and — where the question is about mechanisms — the AIPE. Reporting whether, on average, treatment effects are more positive or more negative at higher moderator values is a small addition to current practice with a real payoff in interpretability.

Discrete treatment, continuous moderator: the mirror image

Take the other canonical setup — $D$ binary, $X$ continuous. Here D-CAME is the only quantity in play, since comparing “at similar treatment intensities” is vacuous when there are only two intensities. But the argument of the paper runs again with the roles of $D$ and $X$ exchanged.

The product-term model assumes $\theta(x)$ is linear in $x$. If it is not, OLS does not return an average of $\theta(x)$ over any theoretically motivated population. It returns a Yitzhaki-weighted average, with weights set by the distance of $X_i$ from the median of $X$ and the local variance of $X$ — and when $D$ and $X$ are correlated, those weights are not innocuous. Same logic, same failure mode, same conclusion: the specification is doing work that has to be defended rather than assumed. This is exactly HMX's point, and their binning and kernel estimators are the right response to it.

What the kernel estimator relies on

Hainmueller, Liu, Liu, Mummolo and Xu (2025) respond to Simonsohn's critique by showing that, once the estimand is properly defined as the CME, their kernel estimator recovers it — even when $Y$ depends on $D$ quadratically and $D$ and $X$ are correlated. Their Figure 2 makes the case with the DGP

$$Y = D^2 - 0.5D + \varepsilon, \qquad (D, X) \sim N\!\left(0, \begin{bmatrix}1 & 0.5\\ 0.5 & 1\end{bmatrix}\right)$$

for which the true CME is $\theta(x) = x - 0.5$, and the kernel estimate tracks it closely. The demonstration is correct. But it is worth being explicit about what makes it work, because the answer is a scope condition rather than a general guarantee.

The kernel estimator relaxes linearity in the moderator. Inside each neighbourhood of $X = x_0$ it still runs a linear regression of $Y$ on $D$, so it localizes in $X$ but never in $D$. What it returns in the limit is a linear projection coefficient:

$$\hat\theta(x) \;\xrightarrow{p}\; \frac{\operatorname{Cov}(D, Y \mid X = x)}{\operatorname{Var}(D \mid X = x)}$$

whereas the estimand is an average derivative, $\theta(x) = E[\,\partial Y/\partial D \mid X = x\,]$. Those two objects are the same thing only under a condition on the conditional distribution of the treatment. Stein's identity gives it exactly: if $D \mid X = x$ is normal, then $\operatorname{Cov}(D, g(D)) = \sigma^2 E[g'(D)]$ for any differentiable $g$, and the projection coefficient is the average derivative. Bivariate normality is not a convenience of the example; it is the assumption doing the work.

For this specific quadratic outcome the requirement is a little weaker. Using $E[D^3 \mid X] = \mu^3 + 3\mu\sigma^2 + \gamma_1\sigma^3$, where $\gamma_1(x)$ is the skewness of $D \mid X = x$,

$$\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(D, Y \mid X=x)}{\operatorname{Var}(D \mid X=x)} \;=\; \underbrace{2\mu(x) - 0.5}_{\theta(x)} \;+\; \underbrace{\gamma_1(x)\,\sigma(x)}_{\text{bias}}$$

So the bias is the conditional skewness times the conditional standard deviation. It vanishes whenever $D \mid X$ is conditionally symmetric — under normality, but also under plenty of non-normal distributions. That is why simply making the treatment non-normal does not reveal the problem: you have to make it conditionally skewed.

The Yitzhaki problem, one level down. The bias term is the same object the paper identifies in the linear interaction model — a linear projection standing in for an average derivative, with weights that come from the geometry of the regression rather than from a theoretical population. The kernel estimator removes it along the moderator dimension and leaves it along the treatment dimension.

A design that separates the two

The accompanying script keeps the outcome equation, the correlation, and the first two moments of $D$ fixed, and varies only the shape of $D \mid X$ across three designs:

  • (a) Normal. $D \sim N(0,1)$ — the HMX benchmark.
  • (b) Copula. $D = \sqrt{3}\,\big(2\Phi(W) - 1\big)$ where $W$ is the standard normal correlated with $X$. Marginally $D \sim U(-\sqrt3, \sqrt3)$, so $E[D] = 0$, $\operatorname{Var}(D) = 1$ and the unconditional skewness is zero — identical to (a) on every summary a reader would check. But conditionally, $D \mid X$ is squeezed against whichever boundary $X$ pushes it toward: right-skewed at low $x$, left-skewed at high $x$, symmetric only at $x = 0$.
  • (c) Symmetric uniform. $D = \rho X + U$ with $U$ uniform. Not normal, but conditionally symmetric. The placebo. 
# (b): marginally uniform, conditionally skewed
S  <- matrix(c(1, rho, rho, 1), 2, 2)
WX <- MASS::mvrnorm(n, mu = c(0, 0), Sigma = S)
W  <- WX[, 1]; X <- WX[, 2]

D <- sqrt(3) * (2 * pnorm(W) - 1)          # D ~ U(-sqrt3, sqrt3)
Y <- D^2 - 0.5 * D + rnorm(n)

# true CAME: W|X=x ~ N(rho x, 1-rho^2) and E[Phi(W)] = Phi(mu / sqrt(1+s2))
came_true <- function(x, rho = 0.5)
  2 * sqrt(3) * (2 * pnorm(rho * x / sqrt(2 - rho^2)) - 1) - 0.5

d <- data.frame(Y = Y, D = D, X = X)

out <- interflex(estimator = "kernel",
                 data = d, Y = "Y", D = "D", X = "X",
                 Ylabel = "Y", Dlabel = "D", Xlabel = "X",
                 vartype = "bootstrap",
                 main = "Kernel model")

Unlike the normal case, the true CAME here is not a straight line: it is S-shaped and saturating, $\theta(x) = 2\sqrt{3}\,\big(2\Phi(\rho x/\sqrt{2-\rho^2}) - 1\big) - 0.5$. Evaluating at the median of the bottom and top terciles of $X$ ($x = \mp 0.967$) and taking the difference gives the D-CAME implied by the design:

Design $\gamma_1(x)$ at $x=0.967$ True D-CAME Kernel limit Recovered
(a) Normal 0.00 1.935 1.935 100%
(b) Copula −0.52 1.977 1.067 54%
(c) Symmetric uniform 0.00 1.935 1.935 100%

The pattern in design (b) is a compression toward the middle. Because $\gamma_1(x)$ has the opposite sign to $x$, the estimated CAME is pulled up where the truth is low and pulled down where the truth is high. At the top tercile the true CAME is 0.489 and the kernel limit is 0.034 — a genuine effect that would be reported as indistinguishable from zero. The estimated interaction is not spurious, but it is roughly half the size it should be.

Two features of this are worth noting. First, the bias does not shrink with the bandwidth: it is present in the $h \to 0$ limit and shows up at essentially the same magnitude at $h = 0.5$, $0.3$ and $0.15$. It is an asymptotic bias, not a smoothing artefact, so more data will not help. Second, design (c) recovers the truth exactly — which is the point of including it. The relevant condition is conditional symmetry (here) or conditional normality (in general), not non-normality per se.

Read this as a scope condition, not a refutation. HMX are right that the CME is a well-defined estimand and that the critiques aimed at their estimators had not specified one. The kernel estimator does target the CME, and under conditional normality it recovers it. What the amendment adds is when to worry: skewed treatments (income, wealth, exposure measures, counts, anything bounded at zero), or bounded treatments where the moderator pushes some groups against a floor or ceiling. Those are common. A useful habit is to plot $D$ within moderator bins and look at the shape, not just the mean.

4.3Controls, and what they do to the estimand

The framework so far treats controls as bookkeeping: they enter additively, and the estimands are defined over the raw distribution of the treatment. Both parts of that deserve more than they currently get, and a future iteration will take them up properly. Two threads.

Controls change the population, not just the precision. Once covariates and fixed effects are partialled out, the comparison being made is no longer “group 1 versus group 0” but “group 1 versus group 0 within the same country-year, the same region, the same covariate cell.” That is usually the right comparison. But it means the distributional discrepancy $\Delta$ from §2.4 should be evaluated within that broader group as well: the difference in treatment intensity that matters is the within-cell difference, not the raw one. Two groups can look very differently exposed overall and nearly identically exposed within country-year — in which case D-CAME and AIPE converge and the whole distinction becomes moot. The reverse can also happen. Whether the distinction bites is therefore a property of the specification, not only of the data.

Additive controls are an assumption. The models here assume $Z$ is additively separable from $D$. When $D$ and $Z$ interact in the true DGP, the model is misspecified and the OLS estimate of the $D$ slope becomes a variance-weighted average of covariate-specific slopes, with weights proportional to the variance of $D$ at each value of $Z$ — the Yitzhaki problem again, now in the covariates. This matters most when the variance of $D$ differs sharply across covariate values. The natural fix is to include $D \times Z$ and average predictions over the empirical distribution of $Z$, marginalizing rather than conditioning. One caution: if $D \times Z$ is included, $Z$ should not then be held fixed. Fixing it moves the target toward a causal-interaction quantity — how treatment effects change under an exogenous manipulation of $Z$ — which is a different estimand from the effect heterogeneity considered here, and one requiring much stronger assumptions.

4.4Discrete multi-valued treatments

The framework assumes $D$ is continuous. Much applied work uses a treatment measured on a ratio scale that takes only a handful of values — days of extreme temperature last week, number of children, years of schooling. Differences between values are substantively meaningful and comparable, so the logic should carry over. It mostly does, but the weights become genuinely awkward.

From derivatives to first differences

When $D$ takes values in $\{d_0, d_1, \ldots, d_K\}$, derivatives are not defined and the natural unit-specific quantities are first differences. The conditional average partial effect of moving from $d_k$ to $d_{k+1}$ for group $x$ is

$$\text{CAPE}_x(d) = E[Y \mid D = d_{k+1}, X = x] - E[Y \mid D = d_k, X = x]$$

and the interactive partial effect is the differential impact of that same move for group 1 relative to group 0:

$$\text{IPE}(d, 1, 0) = \Big\{E[Y \mid D = d_{k+1}, X = 1] - E[Y \mid D = d_k, X = 1]\Big\} - \Big\{E[Y \mid D = d_{k+1}, X = 0] - E[Y \mid D = d_k, X = 0]\Big\}$$

Nothing has gone wrong yet. The unit-specific quantities follow directly from the continuous case.

The weighting problem

The difficulty is in choosing the population — that is, in setting the weights. The standard approach weights each transition from $d_k$ to $d_{k+1}$ by the probability of sitting at the starting level. For a one-unit increase:

$$\tau^{\text{CAME, inc.}}_x = \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} \text{CAPE}_x(d_k)\, p_x(d_k)$$

where $p_x(d_k)$ is the sample share of group $X = x$ with $D = d_k$. This answers a clear question: pick someone at random from group $x$, increase their $D$ by one unit — what is the expected effect?

Note what it does to the observations at the highest observed value. They never appear, because there is no $d_{K+1}$ to move to. So the weights do not sum to one: $\sum_{k=0}^{K-1} p_{D|x}(d_k) < 1$.

Now ask the same question as a one-unit decrease. The effect of moving from $d_{k+1}$ down to $d_k$ is $-\text{CAPE}_x(d_k)$, and the natural weight is now $p_{D|x}(d_{k+1})$ — the share sitting at the starting level of the decrease. Taking the negative to express it as the effect of an increase:

$$\tau^{\text{CAME, dec.}}_x = \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} \text{CAPE}_x(d_k)\, p_{D|x}(d_{k+1})$$

In general $\tau^{\text{CAME, inc.}}_x \neq \tau^{\text{CAME, dec.}}_x$. The underlying unit-specific quantity — the effect of the transition between $d_k$ and $d_{k+1}$ — is identical in both directions. Only the weights change, because the two framings put different people at the starting line.

The consequence is uncomfortable: the CAME, and therefore the D-CAME and the AIPE, depend on an arbitrary framing choice with no theoretical justification behind it. Suppose $D \in \{1, 2, 3\}$ days of extreme temperature. A researcher asking about a one-unit increase has two transitions available, 1→2 and 2→3; because 3→4 is never observed, everyone at $D = 3$ receives zero weight. Ask instead about a one-unit decrease and it is the people at $D = 1$ who drop out.

How to resolve this is beyond the scope of the paper. Averaging the increase and the decrease is one option, though the weights still do not sum to one. The practical recommendation for now is narrower: when the treatment is discrete, state which direction the reported quantity refers to, and check whether the conclusion survives the other framing.
