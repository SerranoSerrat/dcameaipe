Chapter 4

4Further Discussion

This is a superficial discussion on several topics: different ways of concieving non-linear interactions, what controls do to the quantity of interest, and how the framework changes when the treatment is a multi-valued discrete variable.

4.1Three places non-linearity can hide

“Non-linear interaction model” is used loosely for at least three distinct situations, and they call for different fixes. It is worth separating them before going further.

  • Non-linear treatment effects: The effect of one additional unit of treatment depends on how much treatment exposure you have already had. This is what the paper is about, and it is what drives a wedge between D-CAME and AIPE.
  • Non-linear moderation: the effect of the treatment is allowed to vary non-linearly across levels of the moderator. Non-linearity of the treatment effects is not considered, and indeed, many times the treatment is binary. This is the problem Hainmueller, Mummolo and Xu (2019, HMX) set out to solve with binning and kernel estimators. §4.2 takes it up.
  • Non-linear link functions ("Non-linear models"): Logit, probit, and related models impose non-linearity in the treatment by construction. Marginal effects vary with $D$ and with every covariate, even when no product term appears in the index. The estimand logic carries over, but the role of covariates requires separate consideration. This is left for a future iteration of this document.

These are independent. A model can be linear in the treatment and non-linear in the moderator, or the reverse. Crucially, the standard linear interaction commonly used in the literature assumes linearity in both.

4.2Non-linear moderators

The estimand exists, but the summary is missing

HMX and the subsequent literature deliver the conditional marginal effect as a function: $\theta(x) = E[\partial Y_i(d)/\partial d \mid X_i = x]$, plotted across the support of the moderator. In the vocabulary of Chapter 2, that curve is the CAME evaluated at each value of the moderator.

What that literature does not provide is a summary of the interactive effect itself. A plotted CAME curve may show that the effect of the treatment is larger at high $X$ than at low $X$; however, it does not give you a number for “how much larger, on average”. I think researchers could increase the interpretability of their findings by reporting whether, on average, treatment effects are more positive or more negative at higher moderator values. In the same way that IPE can be aggregated to estimate AIPE, a similar procedure could be used in this case.

Discrete treatment, continuous moderator

Indeed, even though the aim of the paper is to explore average moderation effects, non-linearities in the moderation should be taken into account. Consider the other canonical setup --- $D$ binary, $X$ continuous. Here, D-CAME is the only quantity of interest, since comparing “at similar treatment intensities” is redundant when there are only two possible values of the treatment. However, taking non-linearities of the moderator into account is still relevant for estimating the CAME function (see Beiser-McGrath and Beiser-McGrath (2020)).

The discussion: Is assuming non-linearities with respect to the moderator enough?

Simonsohn (2024a), a Data Colada post titled “Dear Political Scientists: Don’t Bin, GAM Instead,” and the accompanying formal article, Simonsohn (2024b, “Interacting with Curves”), argue that, with observational data, HMX’s binning and kernel estimators face a problem: they assume linear treatment effects. When $D$ and $X$ are correlated and either has a non-linear effect on $Y$, the interaction estimate is biased. According to Simonsohn, this is an omitted-variable-bias story, in which the non-linear part of $D$ left out of the model correlates with $D \times X$ precisely because $D$ and $X$ do. Simonsohn’s proposed fix is to replace both binning and kernel with a GAM, considering non-linearities with both, $D$ and $X$.

Hainmueller, Liu, Liu, Mummolo and Xu (2025; HLLMX) reply that the critique never states what “the interactive effect” means. They claim that the quantities of interest is the CAME, $\theta(x) = E[\partial Y_i(d)/\partial d \mid X_i = x]$ --- an average derivative, marginalizing over $D$ and any covariates, conditional only on $X$. They show that the kernel approach that they propose provides unbiased estimates of CAME.

In the first part of the paper, I elaborate a bit on this discussion about different quantities of interest when testing interactions. Chapter 2 draws a line between the effect at the exposure a group actually has — an average over that group’s own treatment distribution, the CAME, and the D-CAME across groups — and the effect at a common exposure, an average over one shared distribution, the AIPE.

My reasoning when I started writing the paper in 2023 was much aligned with Simonsohn approach (even though I had not come about his work at that point). After reading the response by HMX and coauthors, I was compelled that probably CAME and D-CAME were also usueful quantities to be estimated.

At that point, the question was, if D-CAME is the quantity of interest, is assuming a non-linear moderator enough? According to the results of my paper, no. For example, the simulation in §2.5 sets $X$ to a plain binary indicator, group 1 versus group 0. With only two groups, the moderator is introduced "non-parametrically". This simulation shows that not considering non-linearities in $D$ leads to biased estimates of D-CAME.

The next question is, then, why HMX and coauthors find that their kernel approach works? I contend that it may be because of the distribution of $D$ that they assume. To explore this, I consider two distributions:

  • Normal. $D \mid X$ is normal — symmetric at every $x$. This is their benchmark. In particular, $(W,X)$ are drawn jointly normal with correlation $\rho=0.5$. As the authors show, since $E[D \mid X=x] = \rho x$, the true CAME is therefore linear in $x$:

    $\theta_{normal}(x) = 2\,E[D \mid X = x] - 0.5 = x - 0.5$

  • Copula. $D = \sqrt{3}\,\big(2\Phi(W) - 1\big)$, with $W$ and $X$ drawn jointly standard normal with correlation $\rho=0.5$, so that $W \mid X = x \sim N\!\big(0.5 x,\, 1-0.5^2\big)$. Marginally $D \sim U(-\sqrt3, \sqrt3)$. Crucially, even though $D$ is symmetric, $D \mid X$ is skewed: right-skewed at low $x$, left-skewed at high $x$ (symmetric only at $x = 0$). Because $W \mid X=x$ is normal, $E[D \mid X=x]$ has a closed form, and it can be shown that the true CAME can be defined as:

    $\theta_{copula}(x) = 2\sqrt{3}\left( 2\,\Phi\!\left( \dfrac{0.5 x}{\sqrt{2-0.5^2}} \right) - 1 \right) - 0.5$

I simulate a single draw using these DGPs, with $n = 5{,}000$. In particular, I run interflex's kernel estimator, with the package's own cross-validated bandwidth and default settings, and overlay the true CAMEs in red.

Figure 1 shows that when the treatment variable is normally distributed, as the authors demonstrate in their response, the kernel estimation recovers the true CAME (left panel). However, this is not generally the case when the treatment is not normally distributed (right panel).

This happens as well when comparing between groups (i.e., when having a binary moderator). The left panel in Figure 2 shows the same results as Figure TAL in the paper, while the right panel shows the same results but assuming that $D$ is normally distributed.

It is important to recognize that this is only suggestive simulation evidence; no formal proof has been provided. HMX are correct that the CAME is a well-defined estimand and that their kernel estimator targets it when $D$ is normally distributed. This discussion adds a cautionary note that there may be cases where allowing for nonlinearities in the moderator is not sufficient. When the treatment is plausibly skewed within moderator groups --- e.g., income, wealth, exposure measures, counts --- relaxing only the linearity assumptions on the moderator may not be enough. Formal derivations are needed to better understand the conditions under which kernel estimates are unbiased.

4.3Controls, and what they do to the estimand

So far, the controls enter additively into the specifications, and the estimands are defined over the raw distribution of the treatment. Both parts deserve more attention than they currently receive, and in future iterations I will try to address them properly.

Controls may change the quantity of interest. Once the interaction between the treatment variable and other covariates is added, the comparison being made is no longer “group 1 versus group 0” but “group 1 versus group 0 within the same covariate cell.” That may not always be the correct comparison. But if this is the quantity of interest, the distributional discrepancy ($\Delta$) from §2.4 should be evaluated within that covariate cell as well: the difference in treatment intensity that matters is the within-cell difference, not the raw one. Empirically, it should then be assessed how to partial out these covariate differences in the treatment effects and the distribution.

4.4Discrete multi-valued treatments

The framework assumes $D$ is continuous. Much applied work uses a treatment measured on a ratio scale that takes only a handful of values — days of extreme temperature last week, number of children, years of schooling. Differences between values are substantively meaningful and comparable, so the logic should carry over. It mostly does, but the weights become genuinely awkward.

From derivatives to first differences

When $D$ takes values in $\{d_0, d_1, \ldots, d_K\}$, derivatives are not defined and the natural unit-specific quantities are first differences. The conditional average partial effect of moving from $d_k$ to $d_{k+1}$ for group $x$ is

$$\text{CAPE}_x(d) = E[Y \mid D = d_{k+1}, X = x] - E[Y \mid D = d_k, X = x]$$

and the interactive partial effect is the differential impact of that same move for group 1 relative to group 0:

$$\text{IPE}(d, 1, 0) = \Big\{E[Y \mid D = d_{k+1}, X = 1] - E[Y \mid D = d_k, X = 1]\Big\} - \Big\{E[Y \mid D = d_{k+1}, X = 0] - E[Y \mid D = d_k, X = 0]\Big\}$$

Nothing has gone wrong yet. The unit-specific quantities follow directly from the continuous case.

The weighting problem

The difficulty is in choosing the population — that is, in setting the weights. The standard approach weights each transition from $d_k$ to $d_{k+1}$ by the probability of sitting at the starting level. For a one-unit increase:

$$\tau^{\text{CAME, inc.}}_x = \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} \text{CAPE}_x(d_k)\, p_x(d_k)$$

where $p_x(d_k)$ is the sample share of group $X = x$ with $D = d_k$. This answers a clear question: pick someone at random from group $x$, increase their $D$ by one unit — what is the expected effect?

Note what it does to the observations at the highest observed value. They never appear, because there is no $d_{K+1}$ to move to. So the weights do not sum to one: $\sum_{k=0}^{K-1} p_{D|x}(d_k) < 1$.

Now ask the same question as a one-unit decrease. The effect of moving from $d_{k+1}$ down to $d_k$ is $-\text{CAPE}_x(d_k)$, and the natural weight is now $p_{D|x}(d_{k+1})$ — the share sitting at the starting level of the decrease. Taking the negative to express it as the effect of an increase:

$$\tau^{\text{CAME, dec.}}_x = \sum_{k=0}^{K-1} \text{CAPE}_x(d_k)\, p_{D|x}(d_{k+1})$$

In general $\tau^{\text{CAME, inc.}}_x \neq \tau^{\text{CAME, dec.}}_x$. The underlying unit-specific quantity — the effect of the transition between $d_k$ and $d_{k+1}$ — is identical in both directions. Only the weights change, because the two framings put different people at the starting line.

The consequence is uncomfortable: the CAME, and therefore the D-CAME and the AIPE, depend on an arbitrary framing choice with no theoretical justification behind it. Suppose $D \in \{1, 2, 3\}$ days of extreme temperature. A researcher asking about a one-unit increase has two transitions available, 1→2 and 2→3; because 3→4 is never observed, everyone at $D = 3$ receives zero weight. Ask instead about a one-unit decrease and it is the people at $D = 1$ who drop out.

How to resolve this is beyond the scope of the paper. Averaging the increase and the decrease is one option, though the weights still do not sum to one. The practical recommendation for now is narrower: when the treatment is discrete, state which direction the reported quantity refers to, and check whether the conclusion survives the other framing.
