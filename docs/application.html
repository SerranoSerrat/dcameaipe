Chapter 3

3An Application

The paper's worked examples, with the code that produces them. First a stylized data-generating process where we know the right answer, then a real application where the three estimands disagree by a factor of three.

Everything below comes from the replication package at osf.io/8tx6g. Plots returned in res$plot are ggplot objects, so ggsave() works as usual.

3.1A stylized example first

Before the real data, it helps to run the machinery on a DGP where the truth is known. DGP2 (described in the paper's online supplement B) has an inverse U-shaped relationship between $D$ and $Y$ for both groups, and — crucially — the two groups are exposed to $D$ very differently. 

library(haven)
library(dcameaipe)

DGP2 <- read_dta("DGP2.dta")

res_fig_3 <- dcame_aipe(
  data        = DGP2,
  Y           = "Y", D = "D", X = "X",
  estimand    = "DCAME",
  user_spec_0 = 2,          # quadratic for X = 0
  user_spec_1 = 3,          # cubic for X = 1
  inference   = "regular",
  vce         = "robust")

print(res_fig_3$plot)

Note user_spec_0 and user_spec_1: here the functional form is imposed rather than selected, because we know it. That is why inference = "regular" is legitimate — there is no model-selection step whose uncertainty needs to be recovered. In any real application you would leave the specification to be chosen and switch to "crossfit" or "bootstrap".

Both groups show an inverse U-shape. Predicted values are lower for group 1, but the gap shrinks monotonically as $D$ rises and eventually vanishes. The right panel shows why the D-CAME comes out negative and significant: group 1's exposure is concentrated at high values of $D$, where the curve has turned downward, so its CAME loads on the negative part of the slope. Group 0's CAME is close to zero and insignificant. Nothing here is about group 1 responding differently — it is about group 1 being somewhere else on the curve.

Re-running with estimand = "AIPE" and compare = "ALL" makes that explicit: 

res_fig_4 <- dcame_aipe(
  data        = DGP2,
  Y           = "Y", D = "D", X = "X",
  model       = "basis",
  estimand    = "AIPE",
  user_spec_0 = 2,
  user_spec_1 = 3,
  inference   = "regular",
  vce         = "robust",
  compare     = "ALL"       # linear coefficient, D-CAME and AIPE side by side
)

print(res_fig_4$plot)

The middle panel is the new information: the IPEs are large and positive at low $D$, then decline monotonically to zero and lose significance at high $D$. Averaged over the pooled distribution of $D$, the AIPE is positive and significant — the exact opposite sign to the D-CAME.

And the linear model? It returns a coefficient that is not significant. It sits above the estimated D-CAME rather than at it, and the Yitzhaki logic explains why: for group 1 the implicit OLS weights load on low values of $D$, where the variance of $D$ is greater and where the relationship is more positive. Three numbers from one dataset, all defensible arithmetic, all answering different questions — and only two of them answer a question anyone posed.

3.2Robots, education, and anti-immigration attitudes

The empirical illustration revisits a well-known finding: exposure to industrial robots increases support for anti-immigration positions, and education moderates that response. The data are the replication files from Anelli, Colantone and Stanig (2021) — the first seven rounds of the European Social Survey, pooled cross-sections from 2002 to 2015, working-age respondents aged 18 to 65, $N = 154{,}646$.

  • Outcome — anti-immigration attitudes, from self-reported items on economic and cultural concerns and on whether immigrants make the country a better place to live.
  • Treatment — robotization exposure: the routinization of tasks in each occupation multiplied by the change in the number of robots in that country-year, weighted by an individual's probability of working in each occupation given pretreatment age, gender, education and region. The analysis uses the instrument, reporting reduced-form results, and the variable is standardized.
  • Moderator — years of education, kept continuous.
  • Controls — age and gender, plus NUTS2 region and country-year fixed effects. Poststratification weights throughout; standard errors clustered at the region-year level.
robots <- read_dta("application_robots/data_adequated_robots_interactions.dta")

Z_FEs      <- c("nuts", "cntry_year")
Z_controls <- c("age", "female")

set.seed(1234)

results <- dcame_aipe(
  data      = robots,
  Y         = "immig",
  D         = "iv_d13_indshock_fo_std",   # continuous treatment
  X         = "education",
  Z         = Z_controls,
  Z_FE      = Z_FEs,
  wgt       = "pspwght",
  estimand  = "AIPE",
  compare   = "ALL",
  model     = "GAM",
  smooth    = "GCV",
  hist_pv   = "rug_1000",
  inference = "bootstrap", B_boot = 500,
  vce       = "cluster",   cluster = "nuts2_year")

print(results$plot)
This one takes a while. 500 block-cluster bootstrap replications, each refitting the GAM and reselecting the smoothing parameter, on 155,000 observations. smooth = "GCV" rather than "REML" is a deliberate concession to that cost — see §3.3. Set the seed if you want reproducible intervals.

Reading the three panels

Predicted values. Robotization raises anti-immigration attitudes monotonically for both education groups, and the curve is clearly concave: the increase is steep at low levels of exposure and flattens out at high levels. The two curves have much the same shape. The rug underneath shows the thing that matters most for what follows — the highly educated are concentrated at lower exposure.

Interactive partial effects. The IPEs wander above and below zero across the range of $D$ and are not statistically significant anywhere. At comparable levels of robotization exposure, the low- and highly educated respond to robots in much the same way.

The estimands. Here the three approaches part company:

Estimand Estimate Reads as
Linear interaction 0.11 A one-SD rise in exposure raises anti-immigration concerns by 0.11 SD more for the highly educated.
D-CAME 0.33 Three times the linear estimate. Given the exposure each group actually has, a generalized rise in robotization moves the highly educated substantially more.
AIPE ≈ 0, n.s. At similar levels of exposure, education makes no difference to how people respond.

The gap between the D-CAME and the AIPE has a clean explanation, and it is exactly the $\Delta$ term from §2.4. Marginal effects of robotization are decreasing, and the highly educated are less exposed. Their CAME therefore averages slopes drawn from the steep early part of the curve, while the low-educated group's CAME averages slopes from the flat tail. Different positions on a shared curve, not different curves.

The gap between the linear coefficient and the D-CAME is a separate problem. Both are attempts at the same quantity; the linear model recovers a third of it, because forcing a straight line through a concave relationship reweights the slopes by where $D$ has variance rather than by where each group actually sits.

What changes substantively. The descriptive finding survives: as robotization spreads, the educational gradient in anti-immigration attitudes really does widen, and by more than the linear model suggests. What does not survive is the mechanism reading — that education itself changes how people metabolize an economic shock. The AIPE for that claim is zero. Reporting only the linear coefficient would have understated the first conclusion and licensed the second.

3.3A robustness check on the smoothing parameter

GCV is a computationally cheap approximation to leave-one-out cross-validation, and it can undersmooth — a concern mainly in small samples, which is not the situation here. Still, the appendix refits with REML to confirm that the smoothing choice is not doing the work: 

results_robot_reml <- dcame_aipe(
  data     = robots,
  Y        = "immig",
  D        = "iv_d13_indshock_fo_std",
  X        = "education",
  Z        = Z_controls,
  Z_FE     = Z_FEs,
  wgt      = "pspwght",
  estimand = "AIPE",
  compare  = "ALL",
  hist_pv  = "rug_1000",
  model    = "GAM",
  smooth   = "REML")

Point estimates are similar. As a rule of thumb: REML outperforms GCV when the sample is not large, and GCV is worth its slight undersmoothing when REML would make a bootstrap infeasible.
