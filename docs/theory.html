Chapter 2

2Theory in Short

A summary of the argument in the paper: what the two estimands are, when they come apart, and why a linear interaction model may not deliver either of them. Formulas are included for completeness — the intuition survives if you skip them.

2.1One question, two answers

Start with the paper's motivating example. Extreme temperatures damage health, and we want to know whether they damage the health of low-SES people more than that of high-SES people. Suppose the true relationship looks like this: the curve linking temperature exposure to health problems is identical for both groups, but low-SES individuals sit further to the right of it — they are simply exposed more.

Now ask two perfectly sensible questions about this picture:

  • “For exposure as it actually exists, does a further increase in extreme temperatures affect the two groups differently?” Yes — the curve is steeper where low-SES people are concentrated, so a generalized rise in temperatures hurts them more. This is the difference in conditional average marginal effects (D-CAME).
  • “At similar exposure levels, does an increase affect the two groups differently?” No — the curves lie on top of each other, so two people with the same exposure respond the same way regardless of SES. This is the average interactive partial effect (AIPE).

Both answers are correct. They answer different questions. The trouble starts when a paper estimates one and interprets the other — for instance, reporting a significant D-CAME and explaining it with a mechanism (“high-SES individuals can shelter from the heat”) that is a claim about the AIPE, which here is exactly zero. That is how a well-identified estimate turns into an unfounded story.

2.2The building blocks

A theoretical estimand has two parts: a unit-specific quantity and a target population. With a continuous treatment, the unit-specific quantity is the slope of the potential-outcome curve, $\partial Y_i(d)/\partial d$. Averaging it over the units in group $X = x$ gives the conditional average marginal effect:

$$\tau^{\text{CAME}}_x \;=\; \frac{1}{N_x}\sum_{i=1}^{N_x} \frac{\partial Y_i(d)}{\partial d}$$

It is useful to slice this differently. Define the conditional average partial effect, $\text{CAPE}_x(d)$ — the average slope of the curve for group $x$ at treatment value $d$. In the temperature example, $\text{CAPE}_{\text{low}}(2)$ is the marginal effect of more extreme temperatures among low-SES people who have already had two days of them. The CAME is then just a weighted average of CAPEs, with weights given by how common each exposure level is within that group.

The corresponding contrast is the interactive partial effect, the vertical distance between the two groups' slopes at a common value of $d$:

$$\text{IPE}(d) \;=\; \text{CAPE}_1(d) - \text{CAPE}_0(d)$$

In Figure 1, $\text{IPE}(d) = 0$ everywhere — that is the precise sense in which “there is no interaction here.”

2.3The two estimands

D-CAME is the difference between the two groups' CAMEs. Writing it in terms of CAPEs and the group-specific density of the treatment, $f_{D|x}$, makes the weighting visible:

$$\tau^{\text{D-CAME}} \;=\; \tau^{\text{CAME}}_1 - \tau^{\text{CAME}}_0 \;=\; \int_d \text{CAPE}_1(d)\, f_{D|1}(d)\,\mathrm{d}d \;-\; \int_d \text{CAPE}_0(d)\, f_{D|0}(d)\,\mathrm{d}d$$

Each group is evaluated where it actually is. That is a feature, not a bug: it is the right quantity if you want to know how a generalized increase in the treatment would play out across groups given the exposure they already have. But it means D-CAME mixes two things — a difference in slopes and a difference in intensity.

AIPE averages the IPE over a common distribution of the treatment, so both groups are weighted the same way:

$$\tau^{\text{AIPE}} \;=\; \int_d \big[\underbrace{\text{CAPE}_1(d) - \text{CAPE}_0(d)}_{\text{IPE}(d)}\big]\, f(d)\,\mathrm{d}d$$

This is the quantity you want when the claim is about a mechanism: “this group responds differently to the same dose.”

Neither estimand is directly estimable as written, since both involve counterfactual outcomes at treatment values we do not observe. The paper's online supplement A.3 states the identification assumptions connecting these theoretical estimands to their empirical counterparts.

2.4When do they coincide?

Write each group's density as the overall density plus a group-specific deviation, $f_{D|x}(d) = f(d) + \big[f_{D|x}(d) - f(d)\big]$, substitute into the D-CAME expression, and the two estimands separate cleanly:

$$\tau^{\text{D-CAME}} \;=\; \underbrace{\int \big[\text{CAPE}_1(d) - \text{CAPE}_0(d)\big] f(d)\,\mathrm{d}d}_{\tau^{\text{AIPE}}} \;+\; \underbrace{\Delta}_{\substack{\text{distributional}\\ \text{discrepancy}}}$$ $$\Delta \;=\; \int \text{CAPE}_1(d)\big[f_{D|1}(d) - f(d)\big]\mathrm{d}d \;-\; \int \text{CAPE}_0(d)\big[f_{D|0}(d) - f(d)\big]\mathrm{d}d$$

So the two estimands agree exactly when $\Delta = 0$, and there are two ways for that to happen:

  • The groups have the same treatment distribution ($f_{D|1} = f_{D|0} = f$). Then each bracket is zero. Intuitively, if exposure is independent of the moderator, there is no intensity difference to confound the slope difference.
  • Treatment effects are linear ($\text{CAPE}_x(d) = \beta_x$). Then each term becomes $\beta_x \int [f_{D|x}(d) - f(d)]\,\mathrm{d}d = 0$, because both are densities and integrate to one. If the slope is the same everywhere, it cannot matter where you evaluate it.

Only one cell is dangerous — but it is the cell most observational social science lives in. Treatments are rarely distributed identically across groups (that is often why the groups are interesting), and effects are rarely linear over the whole range of a dose.

2.5Why the linear model doesn't settle it

The workhorse specification is

$$Y_i = \gamma + \alpha D_i + \eta X_i + \beta^{L}(D_i \times X_i) + \delta' Z_i + e_i$$

and $\beta^{L}$ is read as “the interactive effect.” The usual defence of this practice runs: the quantity of interest is the D-CAME, and the linear model estimates it. Both halves of that are contestable. Section 2.3 gives one reason the first half may fail — D-CAME may simply not be the question being asked. The second half fails on its own terms.

When treatment effects really are linear for both groups, $\beta^{L}$ is an unbiased estimate of the D-CAME — and hence of the AIPE, since the two coincide. When they are not, Yitzhaki (1996) tells us what OLS actually returns: a weighted sum of the slopes of the $D$–$Y$ relationship, with each observation weighted by the distance of $D_i$ from the median of $D$ and by the local variance of $D$. Those weights come from the geometry of the regression, not from any theory. The result has the right unit-specific quantity attached to the wrong population — so it does not correspond to either estimand.

The paper makes this concrete with a deliberately unforgiving simulation. Group 1 has a piecewise linear relationship — slope $-1$ up to the 75th percentile of $D$, slope $+1$ above it — while for group 0, $Y$ is independent of $D$. $D$ is log-normal and independent of the moderator, so by the table above D-CAME and AIPE are equal, both $-0.5$. There is a single true answer and the linear model has no excuse.

The estimates are not merely noisy around $-0.5$; they are centred on the opposite sign. A researcher running this regression would conclude with confidence that the interaction points the other way. The lesson is not that linear models are always wrong — it is that assuming linear treatment effects is an empirical assumption doing real work, and it deserves the same scrutiny as any other identifying assumption.

2.6What to estimate instead

The alternative is to let each group have its own function of the treatment:

$$Y_i = \mu + \alpha X_i + g_0(D_i) + g_1(D_i) + \delta' \mathbf{Z}_i + e_i$$

with $g_x(\cdot)$ continuously differentiable. Everything then reduces to estimating $g_x$. Adding a quadratic or cubic term is the common fix, but it imposes a shape rather than discovering one. The package offers two more flexible routes:

  • Basis regression with cross-validation. Search over a set of candidate functions — polynomials up to degree 5, cubic splines with up to 6 knots at empirical quintiles — and pick the one minimizing 10-fold cross-validated MSE. It stays linear in parameters, so it is estimated by OLS and plugs straight into instrumental variables, multilevel models, multiple imputation, and lasso-based covariate selection.
  • GAM. Impose no global parametric form; represent $g_x$ as a penalized regression spline with the penalty chosen by REML (or GCV in large samples). More flexible, and it extends naturally to a continuous moderator by fitting a bivariate smooth $g(D, X)$ rather than discretizing.

In simulation both track an oracle that knows the true functional form. When the truth is a polynomial the basis approach is nearly indistinguishable from the oracle; when the truth has a kink — a case neither method is built for — both stay approximately unbiased for both estimands, with wider sampling distributions.

Inference needs care. Choosing the functional form and testing it on the same data is pretexting, and naive standard errors ignore that first step. Two fixes: cross-fitting, which selects the form on one split and estimates on the other (then reverses the roles to recover efficiency), and the bootstrap, which re-runs selection inside every resample. For GAMs the bootstrap is close to mandatory, since the usual intervals treat the smoothing parameter as known and come out too narrow. With clustered data, use a block cluster bootstrap.

Continuous moderators. For the basis approach, interacting the moderator linearly with $g(D)$ is not enough; the paper follows Hainmueller, Mummolo and Xu (2019) in binning:

$$Y_i = \sum_{j=1}^{3}\big\{\mu_j + g_j(D_i) + \alpha_j X_i\big\} G_j + \delta' Z_i + u_i$$

where $G_j$ marks the $j$th tercile of $X$, and the headline results contrast the bottom and top terciles. For GAMs the bivariate smooth handles this directly, but slopes still have to be aggregated over meaningfully defined subgroups — the paper recommends evaluating at the median of $X$ within the bottom and top terciles.

2.7What to report

With a non-linear specification there is no single informative coefficient, so the paper recommends reporting three complementary pieces, each covering the others' blind spots:

Quantity Tells you Doesn't tell you
Predicted values The shape of the treatment–outcome relationship at different moderator levels. Whether the differences in slope are statistically significant.
IPEs Which treatment values drive the interactive effect, and whether it is present across the range. Whether there is an interactive effect on average.
AIPE An overall summary of whether effects differ at comparable treatment levels. The pattern beneath it — very different IPE curves can average to the same AIPE.

Add the predicted-values panel a rug or histogram of $D$ for each group, so readers can see where the data actually are. For between-group comparisons of predicted values, 84 percent intervals are more informative than 95 percent ones — though if the group difference is the object of interest, test it directly rather than eyeballing overlap.

Three guidelines, in one line each. Decide whether your question is about groups as they are (D-CAME) or groups on equal footing (AIPE), and say so. Take non-linear treatment effects seriously even if D-CAME is your target — the linear model may not recover it. And report predicted values, IPEs and the summary estimand together, because no one of them is sufficient.
