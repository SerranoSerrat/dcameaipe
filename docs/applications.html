Where the dCAME/AIPE distinction actually bites — and what changes once you stop assuming the treatment effect is a straight line.

2.1Why the quantity of interest needs to be named

Most applied papers estimate a linear interaction model, look at the coefficient on the product term, and report it as "the" effect of group membership on the treatment-outcome relationship. That coefficient is actually only correct for one specific comparison — and it happens to be a comparison that is well-defined only when the treatment effect is linear. Once the effect curve bends, "the" interaction effect splits into two distinct, equally legitimate questions:

  • How different are the two groups, as they actually experience the treatment? Each group is evaluated at its own typical intensity — this is the difference in Conditional Average Marginal Effects (dCAME).
  • How different would the two groups be if exposed to the same intensity? Both groups are evaluated at a common intensity — this is the Average Interactive Partial Effect (AIPE).

A linear model collapses these into a single number. A non-linear one won't — and the gap between them is often the more interesting finding.

2.2Illustrative case: policy exposure and political behavior

Suppose a researcher asks whether a labor-market shock shifts political attitudes more for economically exposed workers than for insulated ones. If exposed workers also happen to experience more intense shocks on average, then a naive comparison mixes two things together: a genuine difference in sensitivity, and a difference in how hard each group was hit. dCAME answers "who reacted more, given what actually happened to them" — useful for describing the political fallout of an actual event. AIPE answers "who would react more to the same-sized shock" — useful for predicting what happens if the shock's severity changes.

2.3Illustrative case: dosage and treatment effects

In program-evaluation settings where "treatment" is really a dial rather than a switch (hours of exposure, dosage, funding intensity), subgroups frequently receive different average doses simply because of how the program is administered, not because of anything about the outcome model. Reporting only a linear interaction term implicitly assumes the dial has a constant effect per unit — an assumption worth checking, not asserting.

2.4Seeing both quantities at once

dcameaipe makes the comparison in the diagram above concrete: compare = "ALL" fits the flexible model once and reports the linear-model estimate, dCAME, and AIPE side by side, so you can see directly how much the linearity assumption was costing you. 

res <- dcame_aipe(
  data     = mydata, Y = "y", D = "treat", X = "group",
  estimand = "AIPE",
  compare  = "ALL"   # linear model, D-CAME, and AIPE together
)

summary(res)   # one row per estimand
res$plot        # all three, plotted together
